# M4 learning checkpoints

Short answers to the "Learning checkpoint" questions in `docs/milestones/M4-replication.md`, written for someone new to Kafka. Each one links the Kafka behaviour to what the engine does.

## T2 · Replica placement

**Q: Why does Kafka spread the *leaders* of a topic across brokers, not just the replicas?**

Every replica stores the data, but only the **leader** does the work clients notice: all produce requests for a partition go to its leader, all consumer fetches (by default) are served by it, and the followers fetch from it too. A follower only writes what it copies. If one broker led every partition of a busy topic, that broker would take all the network traffic, request handling and disk writes for the topic, while the others sat nearly idle holding copies. Spreading the leaders spreads that load. It also limits the damage of one crash: if a broker that leads a third of the partitions dies, only that third pauses until new leaders are elected (about `broker.session.timeout.ms`, 9 s); if it led everything, the whole topic would stop. That is why `StripedReplicaPlacer` starts each partition one broker further along: partition 0's preferred leader is broker *k*, partition 1's is *k + 1*, and so on (`model/placement.ts`). The first replica in the list is the **preferred leader**. After a failover, leadership drifts to whoever was in sync, and `auto.leader.rebalance.enable` periodically moves it back to the preferred replica (T5), restoring the spread.

## T3 · The high watermark

**Q: A consumer never reads above the HW. What could go wrong if it read up to the leader's LEO?**

Records between the HW and the LEO are on the leader but not yet on every in-sync replica. If the leader crashed right then, the new leader (an in-sync follower) might not have them, so those records would vanish: the old leader truncates them when it comes back (T6). A consumer that had already read them would have acted on records that, as far as the cluster is concerned, never existed. It might have charged a card, sent an email, or updated a database. Worse, the new leader then gives the same offsets to *different* records from new writes, so the consumer's position would point into a different history, and it would silently skip or mis-number records. Reading only below the HW guarantees that everything a consumer sees survives any failure that the ISR can absorb. That is the same promise acks=all gives producers, and both lean on the same mark: an acks=all producer is answered exactly when the HW passes its record (`replication.ts`, DelayedProduce). The cost is a little latency, about two network delays while followers fetch. With the ISR below `min.insync.replicas`, Kafka 4.x freezes the HW altogether ("strict min ISR"), so consumers wait rather than read data that is on too few replicas.

## T4 · ISR shrink

**Q: Why does leaving the ISR *unblock* acks=all producers instead of failing them, as long as the ISR stays at or above minISR?**

acks=all doesn't mean "every replica"; it means "every replica **currently in the ISR**". The HW is the minimum LEO over the ISR, and an acks=all write is answered once the HW passes it. So a follower that stops fetching (crashed, cut off, or just slow) holds the HW still: every acks=all write waits for a copy that is never coming. After `replica.lag.time.max.ms` the leader asks the controller to drop that follower from the ISR. Once that's confirmed, the minimum is taken over the remaining replicas, which already have the records, so the HW jumps forward and every waiting write is answered at once. In the engine you can watch `IsrShrunk`, then `ProduceAcked` 2 ms later (`replication.test.ts`). This doesn't weaken durability as long as the ISR is still at least `min.insync.replicas`: that setting is the floor you chose for "enough copies". If the shrink takes the ISR below it, Kafka stops answering instead. New acks=all writes get `NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS` and the HW freezes (strict min ISR), so nothing is acknowledged on fewer copies than you asked for. The trade-off is availability vs durability: RF 3 with minISR 2 survives one slow or dead broker without blocking producers, and refuses writes when two are gone.

## T5 · Failover time

**Q: After a crash, what decides how long a partition has no leader? Why is controlled shutdown so much faster?**

After a crash nobody tells the controller anything: the dead broker simply stops sending heartbeats. The controller only acts when `broker.session.timeout.ms` (9 s) has passed since the last heartbeat it received. Then it fences the broker, removes it from every ISR and elects new leaders from the remaining in-sync replicas. So the outage of each partition the broker led is roughly the time from the crash to that deadline: between about 7 and 9 s, depending on when the last heartbeat arrived (heartbeats go out every 2 s). In `controller.test.ts` a leader killed at 4.5 s, with its last heartbeat at 4 s, is replaced at 13 s. Producers and consumers wait, retrying, during that time. A controlled shutdown is the broker saying "I'm about to go": it asks the controller first, the controller moves every leadership to another ISR member immediately, and only then does the broker stop. That takes a couple of network round trips (2 ms here), with no gap in leadership and no failed writes. That is why operators restart brokers with a clean shutdown (`kafka-server-stop.sh`, SIGTERM) and never with `kill -9`. Lowering `broker.session.timeout.ms` shortens crash failover, but makes brokers that pause briefly (GC, network hiccup) get fenced for nothing.

## T6 · Truncation by leader epoch

**Q: Why is truncation by leader epoch safer than the old "truncate to my HW" rule? (KIP-101 has the example.)**

A follower learns the HW one fetch *after* the leader moves it, so its own HW is usually a little behind. With the old rule, a restarting replica first truncated its log to its own HW, deleting records that were in fact committed, and then fetched them again. That is harmless only if the leader still has them. KIP-101's example: two replicas A (leader) and B, both with records 0 and 1, but B's HW still says 1. B restarts and truncates offset 1. Before it re-fetches, A crashes and B becomes leader, so record 1, which was committed and acknowledged, is gone. Worse, the next write gets offset 1 on B, while A still has the old record 1; when A returns, the two logs differ at the same offset and nothing detects it. Leader epochs fix both problems. Each leader numbers its term, and each record carries the epoch it was written in. A returning replica doesn't guess from its HW. It asks the current leader "where does my last epoch end on your side?" (in Kafka 4.x, inside the fetch: `divergingEpoch`, KIP-320). It deletes exactly the records the leader doesn't have from that epoch on, and nothing else. In the engine, `truncateFollower` does this, and `RecordsTruncated.acked` tells you how many of the deleted records had been acknowledged: with acks=1 that number can be above zero, with acks=all and minISR ≥ 2 it never is.

## T7 · Retries and idempotence

**Q: Without idempotence, how can a retry write a record twice, and how do sequence numbers stop it?**

A producer only knows a write succeeded when the answer arrives. If the leader appended the record but the answer is lost (the connection drops, the request times out, or leadership moves before the acks=all answer is sent), the producer can't tell "not written" from "written, answer lost". Its only safe move is to send the record again. The broker sees an ordinary new request and appends it a second time, at a new offset: a duplicate that every consumer will read. With idempotence, every record carries the producer's id (PID), an epoch and a per-partition sequence number 0, 1, 2, …. The leader remembers, per producer, the last sequence it appended and the offsets of the last five. A retry carries the *same* sequence. The leader sees "I already have sequence 7 from this producer" and answers with the original offset instead of appending, and the producer counts it as delivered. A sequence that jumps ahead (e.g. 9 when 8 is missing, after a truncation lost record 8) gets `OUT_OF_ORDER_SEQUENCE_NUMBER`: the producer starts a new epoch and renumbers its pending records. That state is replicated with the log, so a new leader can deduplicate too. In the engine, `producer-client.test.ts` shows a record whose answer was lost in a leader move landing exactly once. In Kafka 4.x idempotence is on by default but only with acks=all; with acks=1 the same scenario can write the record twice.

## T8 · Offsets in a topic

**Q: Why does Kafka store offsets in a topic instead of a database? What does a consumer see while its coordinator moves?**

Because Kafka already has the best tool it could want for this: a replicated, ordered, durable log. Committed offsets are tiny, frequent writes ("group g, partition orders-1, next offset 42"), exactly the append-heavy workload a log is good at. Keeping them in `__consumer_offsets` means they are replicated with the same ISR and acks=all machinery as user data, with no external database to run, back up or fail over separately (older Kafka versions used ZooKeeper for this, and it struggled with the write rate). The topic is partitioned by group, and the leader of a group's partition *is* its coordinator. So when that broker dies, coordination moves with the partition's leadership: the new leader replays the partition, rebuilds the group's committed offsets and membership, and takes over. Compaction keeps only the latest record per key, so the topic stays small. During the move a consumer keeps processing the records it already has. Its heartbeats and commits to the old coordinator fail (dropped connection, `NOT_COORDINATOR` or `COORDINATOR_NOT_AVAILABLE`), so it marks the coordinator unknown and asks any broker "who coordinates my group now?" (FindCoordinator). It gets an answer once the partition has a new leader: about `broker.session.timeout.ms` after a crash, milliseconds after a clean shutdown. A commit in flight at that moment fails and is retried by the next auto-commit. A Stable group comes back Stable with the same generation and members, so there is no rebalance (`offsets-store.test.ts`).
